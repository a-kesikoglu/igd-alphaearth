@ABSTRACT AlphaEarth Foundations (AE) embeddings promise accurate mapping from few labels, yet tests with field reference data and geographically separated validation are scarce. This study compared the AE embeddings of 2018 with seasonal Sentinel-2 composites and a Sentinel-1 plus Sentinel-2 stack for five land cover classes, using 10,144 polygons of the Land Use/Cover Area frame Survey (LUCAS) 2018 Copernicus module. # 1. Introduction

Land cover maps derived from satellite imagery underpin agricultural statistics, biodiversity reporting and land use planning, and their quality depends heavily on the amount and representativeness of reference labels. Collecting such labels in the field or by visual interpretation remains the most expensive step of a mapping workflow, which explains the interest in representations that reduce the number of labels required for a given accuracy. Foundation models for Earth observation address this problem by learning general representations from large volumes of unlabelled imagery and transferring them to downstream tasks with light supervision [@lu2025]. A recent benchmark that evaluated such models with reduced training sets showed, however, that pretrained models do not consistently outperform supervised baselines [@marsocci2026].

AlphaEarth Foundations (AE) provides precomputed annual embedding fields at 10 m resolution, in which every pixel is described by a vector of 64 components that summarises optical, radar, lidar and climate observations of the year [@brown2025]. Its developers reported lower errors than other featurization approaches across classification and regression tasks, with the largest margins when labels were sparse [@brown2025]. Random Forest models trained on AE have since matched multisensor Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 stacks for wetland vegetation mapping [@ryan2026], outperformed Sentinel features for vegetation height in disturbed forests [@karim2026] and supported annual land cover mapping in a hyperarid region [@manikandan2026], whereas spectral indices performed better for aboveground biomass in a regenerating tropical forest [@rojas2026].

The interpretation of such comparisons depends on the origin of the labels and on the spatial relation between training and test samples. Labels taken from global products such as ESA WorldCover [@zanaga2022] are model outputs, so agreement with them mainly measures the consistency between two classifiers, and even quantities derived from Sentinel-2 and Dynamic World for a single reservoir vary with compositing, thresholding and postprocessing choices [@taskanat2026a], while good practice in accuracy assessment calls for reference data collected independently of the map [@olofsson2014]. When training and test samples are close in space, random splits reward the reproduction of local conditions and inflate performance estimates, as shown for ecological mapping [@roberts2017; @ploton2020; @meyer2022] and remote sensing classification [@kattenborn2022; @karasiak2022]. Spatial blocking reduces this optimism, but it may become pessimistic when the reference sample is already a probability sample of the mapped region [@wadoux2021; @linnenbrink2024]. In the AE studies above, block based validation was used for vegetation height [@karim2026] and for land cover in Saudi Arabia, where overall accuracy fell from 0.859 under random to 0.815 under blocked cross-validation with WorldCover labels [@manikandan2026]. None of these studies combined field observed labels, controlled label budgets and a test in a separate country.

The Land Use/Cover Area frame Survey (LUCAS) offers such labels. Its 2018 Copernicus module recorded in the field the extent of homogeneous land cover at about 63,000 points across the European Union to support the training and validation of Earth observation products [@dandrimont2021], and LUCAS points have been used to classify Sentinel-2 imagery at continental scale [@venter2021]. This study uses these polygons to compare AE embeddings with Sentinel-2 composites and a Sentinel-1 plus Sentinel-2 stack of the same year for five land cover classes in Spain and Greece, under identical samples, classifiers and test sets. The comparison measures the utility of a ready made multisource representation against representations that users can build from freely available Sentinel data. The contribution of the study lies in a paired comparison across label budgets of 10 to 100 labels per class on field labelled polygons, in the contrast between a spatial holdout within the training country, an external test in a second country and the sensitivity of the estimates to the validation protocol, and in a class level analysis of where the embeddings help and where transfer fails.

# 2. Method

## 2.1. Study area and reference data

Mainland Spain and the Balearic Islands formed the training region and Greece served as an external test region (Figure 1). Both countries combine Mediterranean croplands, sclerophyllous shrublands, grasslands and forests but differ in field structure, topography and class shares. Reference labels were taken from the LUCAS 2018 Copernicus module distributed through Google Earth Engine [@gorelick2017]. During the survey, the extent of homogeneous land cover was recorded in the four cardinal directions up to 51 m from each point, and the resulting polygons were screened for consistency by the data producers [@dandrimont2021]. Of the 63,287 polygons in the catalogue, 11,508 lie in Spain and Greece, 10,890 of these belong to the screened subset, and 10,859 to the level one groups cropland (B), woodland (C), shrubland (D), grassland (E) and bare land (F); artificial land, water and wetlands were omitted because only 31 screened polygons of these groups exist in the two countries. Polygons smaller than 400 m² were removed, leaving 10,144 polygons, 8,359 in Spain and 1,785 in Greece. The field visits took place between April and October 2018, and 89% of them between May and August.

(a) Spain with the 62 locked test blocks of 50 km, the test samples, the training pool and the samples removed by the 5 km buffer. (b) Greece, used only for external testing. Boundaries: US Department of State LSIB 2017.

The target population of the experiment is the set of homogeneous LUCAS reference sites of the five classes in the two countries, in which mixed pixels, field edges and the omitted classes are underrepresented. The reported scores therefore describe classification performance on these sites and are distinct from the accuracy of a complete land cover map, which would require a probability sample of the mapped area [@olofsson2014]. The labels were observed in the field independently of the Sentinel composites and of the classifiers trained here, whereas their independence from the pretraining of AE cannot be established, since European land cover information may have entered the training of the embedding model [@brown2025].

## 2.2. Feature representations and classifiers

AE embeddings of 2018 were taken from the Satellite Embedding V1 annual collection [@brown2025], in which each pixel carries a vector of 64 components with unit length. The Sentinel-2 baseline [@drusch2012] was built from all Level-2A surface reflectance scenes of 2018, masked with Cloud Score+ [@pasquarella2023] where the cumulative clear sky score was below 0.60, using ten bands (B2, B3, B4, B5, B6, B7, B8, B8A, B11, B12). Sentinel-1 ground range detected scenes [@torres2012] acquired in interferometric wide swath mode with VV and VH polarisation were used as distributed in Earth Engine, that is, as calibrated and orthorectified backscatter in decibels without radiometric terrain flattening, combining both orbit directions. For each polygon *i*, features were reduced in Earth Engine on a 10 m sampling grid in geographic coordinates, in which each cell took the value of the underlying native pixel (nearest neighbour) and was weighted by the fraction *a*~p~ of its area inside the polygon,

!EQ \bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{p\in P_{i}}a_{p}\,\mathbf{x}_{p}}{\sum_{p\in P_{i}}a_{p}} | 1

where *P*~i~ is the set of grid cells intersecting the polygon and **x**~p~ the embedding, reflectance or backscatter vector of cell *p*. The polygons contained 3 to 77 grid cell centres (median 43). Composites were computed per pixel as medians over the valid observations of a period,

!EQ \rho_{p,b}^{(Q)}=\mathrm{median}\left\{\rho_{p,b,t}:t\in Q,\;s_{p,t}\geq 0.60\right\} | 2

where *ρ*~p,b,t~ is the value of band *b* at date *t*, *s*~p,t~ the Cloud Score+ value (not applied to Sentinel-1) and *Q* the full year or one calendar quarter (January to March, April to June, July to September, October to December). In every quarter, the whole area of every polygon had at least one clear Sentinel-2 observation, and every polygon had at least 6 ascending and 11 descending Sentinel-1 observations, so all features were complete. The compared feature sets were the quarterly Sentinel-2 composites (S2 seasonal, 40 features), the same composites with quarterly VV and VH medians (S1 + S2 seasonal, 48 features) and the embeddings (AE, 64 features). The annual Sentinel-2 composite, the concatenation of AE and S2 seasonal, single orbit Sentinel-1 stacks and embeddings rescaled to unit length after averaging (the polygon mean has a median norm of 0.991) were evaluated as additional analyses.

The main classifier was Random Forest (RF), which assigns the class receiving most votes from *T* decision trees grown on bootstrap samples with random feature subsets [@breiman2001]. The forests used *T* = 300 trees, the square root of the number of features as split candidates, a minimum of two samples per leaf and class weights balanced within each bootstrap sample. As a linear probe, the evaluation commonly applied to embedding fields [@brown2025], multinomial logistic regression (LR) [@theil1969] modelled the class probabilities as a softmax of linear functions of standardized features, with an L2 penalty of inverse strength 1.0 and the scaler fitted on the training data of each run. Both models were implemented in scikit-learn 1.9.1 [@pedregosa2011], with all hyperparameters fixed before the experiments.

## 2.3. Experimental design and accuracy assessment

Each sample *i*, located by its GPS position or, where this was missing, by the theoretical LUCAS point, was projected to ETRS89 Lambert Azimuthal Equal Area (EPSG:3035) and assigned to a square block of side *L* on a grid anchored at the origin of the projection,

!EQ g_{i}=\left(\left\lfloor\frac{u_{i}}{L}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{v_{i}}{L}\right\rfloor\right) | 3

with easting *u*~i~ and northing *v*~i~, following the block cross-validation concept of Roberts et al. [@roberts2017] and Valavi et al. [@valavi2019]. Of the 248 Spanish blocks of 50 km, 62 (25%, 2,049 polygons) were drawn once with a fixed seed as a locked test set. The draw was accepted under the design condition that every class had at least 30 test and 150 training polygons, which the first draw satisfied, and no model result was inspected before the split was fixed. Training pool samples closer than *δ* = 5 km to any test sample were removed,

!EQ \mathrm{min}_{j\in E}\left\lVert\mathbf{s}_{i}-\mathbf{s}_{j}\right\rVert_{2}\geq\delta,\quad i\in D | 4

where **s** denotes projected point coordinates, *E* the test set and *D* the training pool. This removed 181 polygons and left a minimum distance of 5.7 km between training and test samples (Table 1). Block size and buffer were design choices fixed in advance: 50 km blocks are an order of magnitude larger than the 2 km spacing of the LUCAS grid and still provide 62 test blocks for resampling. The Greek polygons formed an external test set that was never used in training.

!TAB outputs/tables/table1_samples.csv | Table 1. Number of LUCAS 2018 Copernicus polygons per class in the Spanish training pool, the locked Spanish test set, the buffer zone and the Greek external test set | Note: The Spanish columns add up to the 8,359 Spanish polygons.

Training sets of *n* = 10, 25, 50 and 100 labels per class (50 to 500 labels in total) were drawn from the training pool, each budget *R* = 20 times, and in every draw the same polygons were used for all feature sets and both classifiers. The full pool of 6,129 polygons served as a reference for abundant labels (five RF seeds). In a separate validation sensitivity analysis in Spain, RF was trained with 25 and 100 labels per class in ten repetitions while 25% of the samples were held out either at random within classes or as whole blocks of 10, 25, 50 or 100 km, without a buffer, as commonly done in remote sensing studies [@karasiak2022]. The median test set size varied only between 2,085 and 2,116 polygons and the median training pool between 6,244 and 6,274 polygons across these protocols.

Performance was summarised by the macro F1 score, overall accuracy (OA) and balanced accuracy (BA) [@sokolova2009; @brodersen2010]:

!EQ F1_{c}=\frac{2P_{c}R_{c}}{P_{c}+R_{c}},\qquad \mathrm{MF1}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{c=1}^{K}F1_{c} | 5

!EQ \mathrm{BA}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{c=1}^{K}R_{c},\qquad \mathrm{OA}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{c=1}^{K}n_{cc} | 6

where *P*~c~ and *R*~c~ are the precision and recall of class *c*, *K* = 5, *n*~cc~ the number of correctly classified test samples of class *c* and *N* the number of test samples. Macro F1 was the primary metric because it weights the classes equally; small classes such as the 56 Greek bare land polygons therefore influence it as much as large ones, and class F1 scores are reported with their support. Two representations *A* and *B* were compared at budget *n* by the mean paired difference

!EQ \bar{\Delta}_{n}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\mathrm{MF1}_{A,n,r}-\mathrm{MF1}_{B,n,r}\right) | 7

Uncertainty was quantified with a two level cluster bootstrap [@efron1979]. For repetition *r* and test block *g*, a confusion matrix **C**~r,g~ was formed, and in each of 2,000 draws block weights *w*~g~^*^ ~ Multinomial(*G*, 1/*G*) gave

!EQ \mathbf{C}_{r}^{*}=\sum_{g=1}^{G}w_{g}^{*}\,\mathbf{C}_{r,g} | 8

from which macro and class F1 were recomputed; the *R* repetitions were resampled in the same draw with multinomial weights. Block weights were shared by all representations, classifiers and budgets, and repetition weights by all representations and classifiers of a budget, so paired differences were evaluated draw by draw; comparisons across budgets used independent repetition weights. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles gave 95% intervals, with *G* = 62 blocks in Spain and *G* = 112 blocks of 50 km in Greece; intervals with fixed training draws were about 25% narrower and changed none of the comparisons between AE and the Sentinel baselines at equal budgets. To relate the transfer loss to the feature distributions, a dissimilarity index was computed for every test sample *j* after standardising each representation with the training pool statistics, in the spirit of the area of applicability [@meyer2021],

!EQ \mathrm{DI}_{j}=\frac{\mathrm{min}_{i\in D}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}_{j}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}}{\mathrm{median}_{i\in D}\,\mathrm{min}_{k\in D,k\neq i}\left\lVert\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}} | 9

with one nearest neighbour, the self distance excluded and no variable weighting, so that values near one indicate test samples as close to the training data as training samples are to each other. Intermediate tables, per sample predictions and code are available in the project repository (Data Availability Statement).

# 3. Results

On the locked Spanish holdout, AE produced the highest macro F1 score at every budget (Table 2, Figure 2a). With RF it rose from 0.541 with 10 labels per class to 0.638 with 100, compared with 0.487 to 0.583 for S1 + S2 seasonal and 0.473 to 0.572 for S2 seasonal; with 100 labels per class AE also reached the highest OA (0.668) and BA (0.694). Quarterly Sentinel-1 backscatter added 0.009 to 0.014 macro F1 to S2 seasonal in paired comparisons, and stacks built from a single orbit direction scored up to 0.008 lower than the combined stack. The annual Sentinel-2 composite scored 0.019 to 0.039 below S2 seasonal, appending S2 seasonal to AE lowered macro F1 by 0.009 to 0.014 at budgets up to 100 labels per class, and rescaling the embeddings to unit length changed it by at most 0.002.

Scores for all feature sets and for logistic regression are provided in the project repository.

The paired difference between AE and the Sentinel baselines was almost constant between 10 and 100 labels per class (Figure 2c). With RF it amounted to 0.054 to 0.063 against S1 + S2 seasonal and 0.066 to 0.072 against S2 seasonal in Spain, and no 95% interval included zero. LR gave differences of 0.039 to 0.068 with intervals above zero. With the full pool, the RF difference fell to 0.029 (0.003 to 0.057) against S1 + S2 seasonal and 0.043 (0.018 to 0.071) against S2 seasonal, whereas LR kept margins of 0.046 and 0.073. Across budgets, AE with 25 labels per class exceeded S2 seasonal with 100 labels per class under RF by 0.024 (0.007 to 0.041), was comparable to it under LR (0.007, −0.012 to 0.028) and was comparable to S1 + S2 seasonal with 100 labels per class under RF (0.013, −0.003 to 0.029).

(c) Paired macro F1 difference between AE and the two seasonal Sentinel baselines with 95% two level bootstrap intervals over test blocks and training draws.

Performance decreased markedly in Greece for every representation (Figure 2b). With RF and 100 labels per class, macro F1 was 0.463 for AE, 0.393 for S1 + S2 seasonal and 0.381 for S2 seasonal, against 0.638, 0.583 and 0.572 in Spain. The ranking of the representations was preserved, with paired differences of AE of 0.061 to 0.081 against S1 + S2 seasonal and 0.079 to 0.091 against S2 seasonal and intervals excluding zero. With the full pool, the RF differences in Greece shrank to 0.018 and 0.021 and their intervals included zero, while LR retained differences of 0.084 to 0.090.

Within Spain, the validation protocol had only a small effect (Figure 3a). Moving from a random split to blocks of 100 km lowered the macro F1 score of AE from 0.613 to 0.585 with 25 labels per class and from 0.655 to 0.632 with 100, and that of S1 + S2 seasonal from 0.541 to 0.508 and from 0.598 to 0.575, although the median distance from a test sample to its nearest training sample grew from 4 km to 22 km. The gap between the two representations remained stable, whereas the Greek test lowered macro F1 by about 0.19 relative to the random split, seven to eight times the largest effect of block size.

(b, c) Difference in class F1 score between AE and S2 seasonal with 95% two level bootstrap intervals; class support in parentheses.

The gains of AE over S2 seasonal differed between classes and regions (Figure 3b, c). In Spain, with 100 labels per class, class F1 improved most for shrubland (0.102, interval 0.069 to 0.144) and grassland (0.090, 0.055 to 0.124), while bare land showed no clear change (0.013, −0.026 to 0.053). In the row normalised confusion matrices pooled over the 20 training draws, AE reduced the share of shrubland predicted as woodland from 22% to 16%. In Greece, cropland gained most (0.223, 0.146 to 0.283) because the share of croplands predicted as grassland fell from 39% to 24%, grassland showed no gain (−0.008, −0.056 to 0.040), and bare land, with only 56 samples, stayed below an F1 score of 0.16 for both representations, with 57% of these samples predicted as cropland by AE.

The feature distributions explain the transfer loss only in part. For the Sentinel representations, the Greek polygons were as close to the Spanish training pool as the Spanish test polygons, with median dissimilarity indices of 1.00 to 1.02 against 1.01, and fewer than 4% of them exceeded an index of two. In the embedding space, the Greek polygons were farther from the training data (median 1.38 against 1.01). The class composition also changed, with grassland accounting for 31% of the Greek test set against 18% of the Spanish training pool and bare land for 3% against 7%.

# 4. Discussion

The results confirm, with field labels and geographically separated tests, the sample efficiency reported for AE when labels are sparse [@brown2025], and they quantify it against baselines that a user would build from freely available Sentinel data. A nearly constant gain of 0.054 to 0.072 macro F1 in Spain means that, with RF and within the tested budgets, AE trained with 25 labels per class outperformed seasonal Sentinel-2 composites trained with 100 labels per class. This ratio refers to discrete budgets and to RF; with LR, and against the multisensor stack, the cross budget differences were inconclusive, so it indicates the order of magnitude of the saving rather than a fixed factor. The narrowing of the gap with the full pool shows that part of the advantage can be bought back with labels, which agrees with wetland mapping, where AE reached the accuracy of an engineered Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2 and terrain stack [@ryan2026], and with vegetation height mapping in disturbed forests [@karim2026]. Spectral predictors tailored to a narrow biophysical target can still perform better [@rojas2026], and pretrained representations do not dominate in every setting [@marsocci2026]. The stronger result of LR with the full pool is consistent with the linear probes used in the original evaluation of the embeddings [@brown2025].

Because AE assimilates sources that the baselines lack, the experiment measures the utility of representations and does not isolate the contribution of the embedding model itself. The gain is not explained by the quarterly VV and VH backscatter, which added at most 0.018 to the optical composites, while AE kept an advantage of 0.054 to 0.081 over the combined stack; an ablation of the inputs of AE would be required to attribute it to specific sources. AE improved mainly the vegetated classes whose separation depends on structure and seasonality, such as shrubland against woodland and grassland, where the multitemporal and multisensor context summarised in the embeddings [@brown2025] may compensate for overlapping reflectance spectra. The confusion between cropland and bare land in Spain probably reflects fallow and ploughed fields that are bare for much of the year, and the slight loss when Sentinel-2 bands were appended to AE might arise from the dilution of the random feature selection of RF, a mechanism that was not tested directly.

The validation sensitivity analysis qualifies the expectation that random splits strongly inflate performance estimates. Within Spain, random and block estimates differed by less than 0.03 macro F1, much less than the overestimation of up to 28% reported for convolutional networks trained on contiguous image patches [@kattenborn2022] and less than the 4.4 percentage points in OA reported for AE with WorldCover labels [@manikandan2026]. LUCAS points are a stratified subsample of a regular 2 km grid, so random splits of these data come close to the probability sample setting in which design based estimation without spatial blocking is unbiased [@wadoux2021], whereas clustered reference sets behave differently [@karasiak2022; @meyer2022], and estimates interpolated from sparse observation networks, such as machine learning maps of drought severity across Türkiye [@atasever2026], raise similar questions. The external test is more demanding. Macro F1 fell by 0.17 to 0.20 for every representation in Greece although the Sentinel features of the Greek polygons lay within the range of the Spanish training data. This pattern is consistent with a change in the relation between features and classes, together with a shift in class composition towards grassland, rather than with extrapolation into unseen feature space; smaller fields interspersed with grassland and differences in what surveyors recorded as bare land are plausible causes that the present data cannot separate. AE kept its margin under this shift even though its Greek samples lay farther from the training data, yet an absolute macro F1 score of 0.463 shows that Spanish labels alone are insufficient for Greek maps, and distance based diagnostics [@meyer2021; @linnenbrink2024] would not have flagged the loss for the Sentinel features.

The findings are subject to the following limitations. The reference sites favour homogeneous polygons and exclude artificial surfaces and water, the independence of the labels from the training of AE cannot be verified, only one year, two countries and fixed hyperparameters were analysed, and the Greek bare land class had few samples. Extending the design to other years and regions, including the semiarid basins of Central Anatolia, where Earth Engine based analyses have documented vegetation change, desertification and salinity effects [@karkinli2025; @taskanat2026b] but comparable field reference data would be required, and testing whether a small number of local labels recovers the performance lost in transfer are the next steps.

# 5. Conclusion

On field surveyed LUCAS polygons, AlphaEarth embeddings of 2018 improved the macro F1 score of five class land cover classification by 0.054 to 0.063 over a Sentinel-1 plus Sentinel-2 stack and by 0.066 to 0.072 over seasonal Sentinel-2 composites on a spatially separated Spanish holdout when 10 to 100 labels per class were available, with consistent results for Random Forest and logistic regression and in an external test in Greece. Within the tested budgets, a quarter of the labels was enough for AE to exceed the seasonal Sentinel-2 baseline with Random Forest, while the gain narrowed when several thousand labels were available.

For systematically sampled field data such as LUCAS, the choice between random and block validation within a region mattered much less than testing in a different region, where performance fell by 0.17 to 0.20 macro F1 for embeddings and Sentinel features alike without being signalled by feature space distances. Studies that use embedding fields for operational mapping should therefore report performance in the target region with independent reference data.
